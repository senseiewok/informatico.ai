Act I — The Challenge, Prize Origin Story & Historical Turning Points (B.03)

In Plain Words · Extra-Easy Explanation

Think about the modern buildings you walk through every day: schools, offices, libraries, and apartment towers. In almost all of them, the heavy floors beneath your feet are thick, flat slabs of solid poured concrete. Concrete is extraordinary at resisting compression, but manufacturing cement produces massive carbon emissions. What few people realize is that inside any flat concrete floor, most of the material is not actually working. When a floor carries weight, the top surface gets squeezed and the bottom gets stretched, while the entire thick middle zone merely acts as dead weight that the building must hold up against gravity. What if we could carve away all that lazy, inactive concrete—just like nature hollows out bird bones and human thigh bones—leaving behind only a graceful lattice of arching ribs where forces actually travel? And what if, instead of releasing greenhouse gases, the curing concrete permanently trapped captured carbon dioxide as solid stone? That is the breakthrough of this bounty: cutting slab weight by nearly two-thirds while locking carbon away forever.

How Human Pioneers Reached This Frontier

How Human Pioneers Reached This Frontier: From 1904 Bone & Truss Geometry to SIMP & Mineralized Concrete

In 1904, an Australian mechanical engineer named Anthony George Maldon Michell asked a question that still haunts every structural engineer: what is the lightest possible frame that can carry a given load between supports? Working purely with calculus on paper, Michell proved that an ideal minimum-weight structure never uses solid rectangular blocks; instead, it weaves curving, orthogonal fan-like ribs that follow the exact paths of tension and compression—looking remarkably like the porous trabecular bone inside a human femur or a bird's wing. Half a century later, Italian master builder Pier Luigi Nervi proved Michell right in poured concrete, sculpting breathtaking ribbed ceilings for exhibition halls in Turin and Rome that saved massive amounts of material. Yet because crafting custom curved wooden molds by hand took months of specialized carpentry, twentieth-century construction abandoned Nervi's ribs in favor of cheap, flat, solid concrete slabs—wasting up to two-thirds of every floor plate as dead, inactive weight.

The turning point arrived between 1989 and 2001 when Danish mathematicians Martin Bendsøe and Ole Sigmund invented Solid Isotropic Material with Penalization (SIMP) and published a legendary 99-line computer code that allowed any finite-element solver to carve solid blocks into optimal bone-like ribs automatically. In the 2020s, two final pieces clicked into place: open-source PDE engines like FEniCS/dolfinx paired with robotic 3D concrete extrusion eliminated expensive wooden molds, while accelerated carbonate mineralization (injecting captured CO₂ gas during curing so it turns permanently into solid limestone CaCO₃ inside the slab) turned concrete from a climate liability into a permanent carbon sink—driving floor emissions below the 45 kg CO₂e/m² IStructE A+ and $100M XPRIZE Carbon Removal threshold.

Cement production generates roughly 8% of global carbon dioxide emissions, and in a typical commercial building frame, flat two-way floor slabs consume more concrete than columns, shear walls, and foundations combined. Because a solid slab carries bending through a thin compression flange at the top and tension reinforcement at the bottom, up to 65% of its interior volume acts as parasitic dead load.

Martin Bendsøe and Ole Sigmund's Solid Isotropic Material with Penalization (SIMP, DOI DOI 10.1007/s001580050176 [Scite ↗]) turns structural design into a partial-differential-equation optimization problem solvable in open-source FEniCS/dolfinx. By penalizing intermediate densities E(ρ_e) = E_min + ρ_e^p (E_0 - E_min) with p = 3 and filtering sensitivities across a mesh radius r_min, the solver carves branching isostatic ribs along Michell's principal tension-compression trajectories. When robotic 3D extrusion (Folio E.10) prints those voided ribs and cures them via accelerated carbonate mineralization (CaO + CO₂ → CaCO₃), net embodied carbon drops from 148.0 kg CO₂e/m² to 39.40 kg CO₂e/m²—clearing the 45.0 kg CO₂e/m² IStructE SCORS A+ and XPRIZE Carbon Removal mineralization benchmark.

Structural engineers have understood since the era of Robert Maillart and Pier Luigi Nervi that material near the neutral axis carries negligible stress, yet manual formwork complexity historically made curved ribs economically impractical.
