Act I — Cool Fact & Human Story: Why Quantum Computers Could Break Today's Internet Locks, and Why Cryptographers Turned to 768-Dimensional Lattices

In 1994 Peter Shor, then at AT&T Bell Laboratories, published a quantum algorithm for factoring integers and computing discrete logarithms in polynomial time. If a large enough fault-tolerant quantum computer is ever built, it could break the RSA and elliptic-curve systems that protect much of today's internet traffic, and data recorded now could be decrypted later. That prospect led cryptographers toward problems on high-dimensional lattices, which no known quantum algorithm solves efficiently. Proof assistants such as Lean offer a different kind of assurance: they check each step of a formal proof mechanically, though only for the statements someone has formalised.

In Plain Words · Extra-Easy Explanation

Every time you log into your bank, send a text message, or sign a digital document on your computer, your privacy depends on a clever mathematical padlock. Today's locks work like a one-way street: multiplying two gigantic prime numbers together is lightning fast, but unscrambling their product back into the original primes takes a conventional supercomputer thousands of years. A large future quantum computer could crack those prime-number locks. To keep our digital world safe, scientists designed a completely new type of padlock based on high-dimensional geometric grids called crystal lattices. Imagine a giant egg carton floating in hundreds of dimensions, hidden behind a dense cloud of fog. Dropping a marble into one specific valley is easy, but finding which valley it landed in without the secret blueprint is believed to be extremely hard, even for a quantum computer. Machine-checked proof systems can inspect mathematical arguments step by step, but they check only what has been formalised; no tool guarantees that a lock will never fail.

In 2024 the National Institute of Standards and Technology published FIPS 203, the Module-Lattice-Based Key-Encapsulation Mechanism (ML-KEM). It belongs to the family of schemes built on Oded Regev's Learning With Errors problem (Journal of the ACM, DOI 10.1145/1568318.1568324 [Scite ↗]), and open-source implementations exist, such as github.com/open-quantum-safe/liboqs. FIPS 203 fixes n = 256 and q = 3329 and defines three parameter sets, ML-KEM-512, -768 and -1024, in NIST security categories 1, 3 and 5. Its security rests on the presumed hardness of module lattice problems; Regev showed that plain LWE is at least as hard, under a quantum reduction, as certain worst-case lattice problems, but no one has proved that these problems are hard. Separately, the public repositories github.com/anthropics/formal-math and github.com/anthropics/fermats-last-theorem state in their READMEs, read on 2026-11-14, that they contain Lean 4 formalisations; this site has not built them. The parent folios of this synthesis cite Andrew Wiles's modularity paper (Annals of Mathematics, DOI 10.2307/2118559 [Scite ↗]), the FunSearch program-search paper (Nature, DOI 10.1038/s41586-023-06924-6 [Scite ↗]) and Ryan Williams's faster all-pairs shortest paths via circuit complexity (SIAM Journal on Computing, DOI 10.1137/15M1024524 [Scite ↗]). None of these formalisations makes a cryptographic implementation bug-free.

Cryptography protects real infrastructure, and an error in a proof or in code can matter. Paper proofs are reviewed by people who sometimes miss edge cases. Formalising a reduction in Lean 4 removes one source of doubt: a theorem whose proof uses no sorry and no extra axioms has been checked by the kernel, though the statement may still not say what was intended. Moving from factoring to module lattices is the current best judgment of how to resist quantum attackers; it is a well-studied bet, not a guarantee.

Act II — Cross-Field Intuition: A 768-Dimensional Egg Carton in Thick Fog + The Unbribable Lean 4 Referee

Everyday Analogy & Interactive Controls Primer

Everyday Analogy: Imagine an egg carton that stretches across seven hundred sixty-eight dimensions, covered in an impenetrable bank of fog. The public key is like dropping a tiny marble into one secret indentation near a grid point, then giving the whole carton a slight nudge (+e). Without the secret map, a searcher, as far as anyone knows even one with a quantum computer, faces an astronomical number of similar valleys. But with the private trapdoor, the rightful owner reaches straight through the fog and retrieves the marble in a microsecond.

How the Two Sliders Work in Plain Words: To understand how this post-quantum lock operates, consider two primary adjustments on our cryptographic dashboard. The first adjustment controls the geometric dimension or rank of the polynomial crystal lattice, toggling between rank two (ML-KEM-512, NIST security category 1), rank three (ML-KEM-768, category 3) and rank four (ML-KEM-1024, category 5). As you increase this rank, the lattice expands from 512 up to 1,024 dimensions, and the cost of the best known attacks rises steeply. The second adjustment regulates the width of the binomial noise fog injected into the public key. If the fog is too thin, clever algorithms might pinpoint the underlying lattice grid; if the fog is too thick, even the legitimate receiver might experience a rare decryption error. By dialing these controls in unison, you can see why FIPS 203 settles on its parameter sets: enough noise to hide the secret, little enough that decapsulation failures stay extremely rare. The dashboard curves are a teaching model, not a security estimate.

Why can a quantum computer factor integers efficiently while lattice problems are believed to resist it? RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography rest on abelian groups whose hidden periodicity a^(x+r) = a^x mod N can be extracted by Shor's quantum Fourier transform. Module-LWE works over the ring R_q = Z_q[X]/(X^256 + 1) with q = 3329 and module rank k = 3 in ML-KEM-768. The public key b = A s + e (mod q) hides the secret vector s behind small centered binomial noise e. Recovering s from (A, b) can be phrased as a problem on a lattice of dimension 768, and the best known attacks, classical or quantum, use lattice reduction and sieving with no exponential quantum speedup known. That is evidence, not proof, of hardness. Live parameter coupling: k = 3 under governing law — in plain words: module learning-with-errors hides a secret vector behind modular matrix multiplication plus small noise at prime modulus 3329 and dimension 256 in NIST FIPS 203.

Regev (2009) showed that solving average-case LWE would let one solve certain worst-case lattice problems with a quantum algorithm; analogous results for module and ring variants underlie schemes such as ML-KEM. In the folio's dashboard, raising the module rank k from 2 to 4 raises the dimension of the underlying lattice from 512 to 1024, which makes the known lattice-reduction attacks much more expensive. FIPS 203 fixes the noise parameters (eta1 = 3 for ML-KEM-512 and 2 for the others; eta2 = 2) and lists decapsulation failure rates of 2^-138.8, 2^-164.8 and 2^-174.8 for the three parameter sets. Earlier editions also described an EightSquare.lean proof that an eight-square bilinear map needs only 28 products; no file of that name appears in the anthropics/formal-math file tree read on 2026-11-14, and that claim is withdrawn.
