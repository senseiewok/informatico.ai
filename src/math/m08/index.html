Act I — From Hermann Grassmann's Laws of Color Mixing to Erwin Schrödinger's Riemannian Color Metrics

In 1853 Hermann Grassmann, a teacher in Stettin, published a study on color mixing in Annalen der Physik und Chemie responding to optical experiments by Hermann von Helmholtz. Grassmann separated every color impression into three mathematical moments: brightness, hue, and colorless admixture. Additive light matching therefore behaves like a linear map into three coordinates. Sunlight spans a continuous rainbow of wavelengths, yet human daylight vision compares lights through only three cone classes. Between 1891 and 1892, Helmholtz proposed a three-dimensional differential line element to convert small matching thresholds into geometric distances. After three decades of neglect, Erwin Schrödinger revived that geometric program in three 1920 memoirs in Annalen der Physik. He separated lower affine color matching from a higher Riemannian metric where shortest paths between hues may curve like routes across a globe.

In Plain Words · Extra-Easy Explanation

Have you noticed a sweater match a pair of pants under bright store lamps, yet clash outdoors in afternoon sunlight? Or mixed blue and red paint on a palette, hoping for violet, and gotten a dull gray instead? Those surprises start in the retina. Our eyes do not record each wavelength of light on its own. Three cone classes compress an infinite-dimensional light spectrum into three signals. Many distinct wavelength mixtures can trigger identical cone totals under one lamp while separating under another. Perceptual spacing across colors is uneven as well. Equal steps in digital red, green, and blue values do not look equally spaced to a human observer, and straight digital blends might drift in hue. Curved threshold models suggest why local steps bend, with one important caveat at large intervals.

Projecting an infinite-dimensional wave space onto three signals leaves an infinite-dimensional nullspace, so distinct spectra match whenever their difference lies in that kernel (Grassmann 1853, DOI 10.1002/andp.18531650505 [Scite ↗]). In 1920, Schrödinger distinguished affine matching ("lower color metric") from a Riemannian line element ("higher color metric") anchored in just-noticeable steps (DOI 10.1002/andp.19203682202 [Scite ↗]; Roberti & Peruzzi 2023, DOI 10.1007/s00407-023-00317-x [Scite ↗]). In 1942, David L. MacAdam recorded over 25,000 trials for one observer at 15 millilamberts, plotting 25 ellipses that no projective map of the 1931 diagram turns into equal circles (DOI 10.1364/JOSA.32.000247 [Scite ↗]).

W. S. Stiles (1946, DOI 10.1088/0959-5309/58/1/305 [Scite ↗]) modified Helmholtz’s line element (Peruzzi & Roberti 2023, DOI 10.1007/s00407-023-00304-2 [Scite ↗]) using limiting Fechner fractions, noting remaining gaps against MacAdam's data. Industrial colorimetry later adopted CIEDE2000 on CIELAB with lightness, chroma, hue, and blue-rotation weights (Luo, Cui & Rigg 2001, DOI 10.1002/col.1049 [Scite ↗]). Yet Bujack et al. (2022, DOI 10.1073/pnas.2119753119 [Scite ↗]) showed that Riemannian metrics overestimate large color intervals because perceived differences show diminishing returns below summed small steps.
