Act I — The Quadratic Wall in Polynomial Time and the Rise of Fine-Grained Complexity

In the early 1970s Stephen Cook and Richard Karp showed how to separate problems that can be solved in polynomial time from NP-complete ones, which are believed to need far more. Polynomial time is not always fast enough: an algorithm that takes cubic time on a graph with n nodes needs about n^3 steps. Fine-grained complexity asks which polynomial exponents can be improved. Its central conjectures concern 3SUM, All-Pairs Shortest Paths (APSP) and the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis. In 2010 Virginia Vassilevska Williams and Ryan Williams showed that APSP is subcubic-equivalent to several other problems (published in the Journal of the ACM, 2018, DOI 10.1145/3186893), and in 2018 Ryan Williams published an algorithm that takes a factor 2^(Ω(√log n)) off the cubic running time for APSP.

In Plain Words · Extra-Easy Explanation

In plain words, this research is about the shortest route between every pair of towns in a large road network. Checking every possible connection one by one takes a number of steps that grows roughly with the cube of the number of towns, which becomes a problem for big networks. For a long time nobody knew how to do substantially better than a modest saving. Williams showed how to save a factor that grows slowly with the size of the network, and a recent repository README states a much larger polynomial improvement, which this site has not checked. This folio makes no claim about navigation apps, logistics or genetics.

Ryan Williams's paper in the SIAM Journal on Computing (2018, DOI DOI 10.1137/15M1024524 [Scite ↗]) gives a randomized algorithm for the min-plus product, and so for APSP, that runs in time n^3/2^(Ω(√log n)) on the real RAM, where earlier algorithms took O(n^3/log^c n) for c ≤ 2. It applies Razborov-Smolensky polynomials to reduce the min-plus product to a relatively small number of rectangular matrix products over F_2, each computed by a method due to Coppersmith. The README of the repository anthropics/formal-math lists a 3sum-apsp project whose stated result, due to Alman and Vassilevska Williams (arXiv:2610.06783), is that 3SUM can be solved in O(n^1.9992) steps and APSP in O(n^2.99942) steps on integers of polynomial size; a later preprint by Chan (arXiv:2610.42012) refers to this result as announced by those authors. Both are reported statements: this site has not built the project or checked the proofs. Separately, Lightman et al. (arXiv:2305.20050) compare outcome supervision, which rewards only a final answer, with process supervision, which rewards each step, for models solving problems from the MATH dataset.

Fine-grained complexity ties the exact exponent of many problems to a few conjectures. Vassilevska Williams and Williams showed that APSP is subcubic-equivalent to several other problems (DOI DOI 10.1145/3186893 [Scite ↗]), and Gajentaan and Overmars showed that many problems in computational geometry are 3SUM-hard, meaning a truly subquadratic algorithm for any of them would give one for 3SUM (DOI DOI 10.1016/0925-7721(95)00022-2 [Scite ↗]). A statement that refutes one of these hypotheses is a very large claim and needs independent expert review. The folio sets this beside process supervision only as an analogy: in both cases a long chain is only as good as its steps.

Act II — How It Actually Works: Razborov–Smolensky Polynomials + Step-Level PRM

Everyday Analogy & Interactive Controls Primer

Everyday Analogy: Imagine planning connecting flights across four thousand regional airports during a holiday storm. If you inspect every single layover combination one by one, you must evaluate billions of possibilities, and your computer freezes. Instead, an experienced travel coordinator groups neighboring airports into regional hubs. By checking weather patterns across whole regional corridors at once, you rule out thousands of delayed connecting routes in a single pass, finding the fastest itinerary without getting overwhelmed.

How the Two Sliders Work in Plain Words: To see how this batching shortcut works in practice, you can explore two intuitive control knobs. The first knob controls the grouping size, deciding how many regional cities or logical steps are bundled into each algebraic package. If you keep the grouping parameter at zero, the computer checks each connection individually, forcing the calculation into a slow, grinding crawl. But as you dial the grouping knob higher, the computer translates multiple comparisons into algebraic polynomials, evaluating hundreds of routes in parallel. The second knob measures constraint density, tracking how many overlapping rules or scheduling limits crowd into the network. When constraints are sparse, paths remain open and flexible. However, as constraint crowding approaches the critical boundary, conflict points multiply rapidly. The two knobs drive an illustration with assumed numbers; they are not measurements of any algorithm.

The classical Floyd–Warshall algorithm evaluates the min-plus distance product (A * B)_{ij} = min_k (A_{ik} + B_{kj}) across n vertices, using about n^3 comparisons. Ryan Williams applied Razborov–Smolensky polynomials to reduce the min-plus product to a relatively small number of rectangular matrix products over F_2 (SIAM Journal on Computing, DOI DOI 10.1137/15M1024524 [Scite ↗]), which gives a running time of n^3/2^(Ω(√log n)). That is an asymptotic statement with an unspecified constant, so it does not say what the speedup is at n = 4,096; the divisor 14.8 on this page is an assumed illustrative value. Separately, multi-step reasoning has an analogous issue: if each of 20 steps is valid independently with probability p, the whole chain is valid with probability p^20. With p = 0.9566 that is about 41.2%, and with p = 0.99723 about 94.6%. These are the two numbers the page shows; they come from the assumed per-step probabilities and are not measurements of any outcome-supervised or process-supervised model. Live parameter coupling: d_poly = 5 under governing law .

Fine-grained reductions tie together three conjectures: the 3SUM hypothesis, the All-Pairs Shortest Paths conjecture and the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis. Under those conjectures, computing the min-plus matrix product was believed to need about n^3 operations up to sub-polynomial factors. Williams does not contradict that belief, since his saving is a factor of 2^(Ω(√log n)), which is smaller than any polynomial in n. The README of the 3sum-apsp project states a polynomial saving for APSP, which would contradict the APSP conjecture; this site has not checked that claim. The page's sliders are illustrative controls: they are not parameters of Williams's algorithm and they do not model the satisfiability threshold.
