Act I — How Thin Masonry Vaults Stand in Compression

In 1864 James Clerk Maxwell published a short paper on reciprocal figures and diagrams of forces. He observed that when the drawing of a plane frame is the projection of a polyhedron with flat faces, the frame can carry a balanced set of internal forces, and in a longer paper of 1870 he read that polyhedron as a stress function. Masonry vaults depend on the same idea of balance: stone and tile resist squeezing far better than pulling, so a vault stands when its forces can flow through it in compression. In the United States, Rafael Guastavino and his son, who emigrated from Spain in 1881, built hundreds of thin timbrel tile vaults (López et al. 2023, DOI 10.3390/buildings13082052). This folio follows Maxwell's idea into three dimensions, where force diagrams become polyhedra.

In Plain Words · Extra-Easy Explanation

In plain words, think about how stone and concrete work. If you try to pull a stone apart like a piece of taffy, it cracks and fails almost immediately. But if you squeeze a stone tightly between your hands, it can support immense weight without breaking. Standard modern floors are built as flat concrete slabs. Because flat slabs bend and sag in the middle under gravity, the bottom half gets stretched, forcing builders to bury thousands of pounds of expensive, rust-prone steel rebar inside the concrete. Master vault builders avoided this problem completely by arching the floor upward into a shallow dome. When gravity presses down on an arch, the stone blocks wedge tightly against one another, funneling all the weight downward into pure compression. By using modern computers to shape these curved vaults in three dimensions, architects can shape floors of tile, brick or plain concrete that need little or no steel in the vault itself. One published unreinforced funicular floor, the Hi-Lo floor, uses about one-third of the material of a conventional flat concrete slab (Küpfer et al. 2025, DOI 10.1088/1755-1315/1554/1/012083). The thrust still has to be taken by ties or supports.

In Folio A.01 we traced Robert Hooke's 2D hanging chain and Jacques Heyman's plastic limit theorems (The Stone Skeleton, 1995, DOI DOI 10.1017/cbo9781107050310 [Scite ↗]). But real architectural spaces are three-dimensional, curved, and pierced by stairwells where simple two-dimensional slices fail. Maxwell's reciprocal figures (1864, 1870) linked plane frames in equilibrium to polyhedra and stress functions; C. R. Calladine (1978, DOI DOI 10.1016/0020-7683(78)90052-5 [Scite ↗]) revisited Maxwell's rule for counting the stiffness of frames; and Akbarzadeh, Van Mele, and Block (2015, DOI DOI 10.1016/j.cad.2015.01.006 [Scite ↗]) extended graphic statics to three dimensions with polyhedral force diagrams. Philippe Block's research group at ETH Zurich also develops the open-source COMPAS framework (github.com/compas-dev/compas), and built the dry-assembled cut-stone Armadillo Vault for the 2016 Venice Architecture Biennale. In this folio we pair such compression shells with a passive daytime radiative sky-cooling coating on the extrados (Raman et al. 2014, DOI DOI 10.1038/nature13883 [Scite ↗]; Mandal et al. 2018, porous P(VdF-HFP) polymer, DOI DOI 10.1126/science.aat9513 [Scite ↗]) and link them to neutral STEP AP242, 3MF, DXF, and GLB voussoir geometry in Toolbox X.03 (/toolchain/x03/). The pairing is a design study.

For centuries, master masons relied on lodge rules of thumb and geometric templates to size stone arches and buttresses. While these empirical rules produced magnificent structures, they required immense intuition and could not safely predict how irregular, freeform, or asymmetric vaults would behave under complex live loads. When twentieth-century builders transitioned to flat reinforced-concrete slabs, they substituted computational certainty for material efficiency: pouring massive, heavy flat slabs packed with tensile steel was simpler to calculate than complex masonry curves. Floor slabs use a large share of the concrete in a multi-storey building, and steel reinforcement can corrode where moisture and carbonation reach it. Unreinforced funicular floors aim to use less material and avoid reinforcement in the vault; one published system uses about one-third of the material of a flat slab (Küpfer et al. 2025, DOI DOI 10.1088/1755-1315/1554/1/012083 [Scite ↗]). How durable such floors are over a building's life is a question this folio does not answer.

Act II — Cross-Field Intuition: Soap-Foam Dual Polyhedra & Concave Stress Bowls

Everyday Analogy & Interactive Controls Primer

Everyday Analogy: Think of a cluster of soap bubbles resting in a wire frame: every paper-thin soap film pulls in perfect physical balance because each bubble cell is completely closed on all sides. Now imagine taking that cluster of soap bubbles and flipping it upside down like an umbrella: every gentle tension pull in the soap film converts into pure, rock-solid compression between stone blocks. If the dual bubble cells close, the stone ceiling simply cannot fall down.

How the Two Sliders Work in Plain Words: To design a compression-only stone vault using this soap-bubble duality, you work with two primary structural knobs. The first knob controls the scale of the reciprocal force crystal, which acts like an invisible mathematical shadow of the building. As you enlarge this dual crystal, the internal forces pushing through the stone ribs increase, showing you how much horizontal thrust the building's perimeter walls and foundations must push back against to keep the vault stable. The second knob adjusts the crown rise of the vault relative to the distance it spans across the room. If you dial this knob down toward a very shallow, almost flat arch, the vault exerts enormous outward thrust against the support walls, requiring heavy abutments. As you increase the crown rise and arch the vault higher, the downward forces flow more steeply into the ground, reducing horizontal thrust and allowing the floor to be built remarkably thin and light without any steel reinforcement.

Imagine two linked three-dimensional geometric networks displayed side by side in the open-source compas-dev/compas framework: the Form Diagram Gamma (representing the physical geometry of the unreinforced stone or tile vault ribs) and the Reciprocal Force Diagram Gamma_star (a cellular cluster of convex polyhedral cells; Akbarzadeh et al. 2015, DOI DOI 10.1016/j.cad.2015.01.006 [Scite ↗]). Every physical strut e_i in the vault is strictly perpendicular to a corresponding polygonal face f_i_star in the dual force diagram, and the area of that face A_i_star is proportional to the axial force N_i in the strut. The outward area vectors of any closed polyhedron sum to zero, which is why a closed cell expresses the balance of forces at a node. In-plane membrane stresses satisfy sigma_xx = Phi_yy, sigma_yy = Phi_xx, and sigma_xy = -Phi_xy on the Airy stress surface Phi(x, y): negative-definite curvature (Phi_xx < 0, Phi_yy < 0, det(H_Phi) = Phi_xx*Phi_yy - Phi_xy^2 > 0) means the in-plane stresses of the model are compressive everywhere. The value det(H_Phi) = +1.84 shown on this page is illustrative; no Airy surface was solved for a real vault. Live parameter coupling: ζ = 1.15 under governing law .

## The governing relation
`σ_xx = Φ_yy · σ_yy = Φ_xx · det(H_Φ) > 0 · N_i ∝ A_i^* · q_net(8–13 μm) (folio design target ≥ 80 W/m²)`

## Rules the essay derives
1. Check 3D nodal equilibrium by requiring every polyhedral cell c_v^* in the reciprocal force diagram Gamma^* to close within a numerical tolerance (for example ||sum F_i|| < 1e-12 kN).
2. Check that the model stress field is compressive by testing the Airy stress Hessian: Phi_xx < 0, Phi_yy < 0, and det(H_Phi) = Phi_xx*Phi_yy - Phi_xy^2 > 0. This is a model check for one load case, not a structural assessment of a real vault.
3. Evaluate daytime radiative sky-cooling net flux q_net = q_rad(T_s) - q_atm(T_a) - q_solar*(1 - R_solar) - h_c*(T_a - T_s); treat q_net >= 80 W/m^2 at T_a = 303.15 K and Delta T_eq <= -4.5 C as this folio's own design targets, and report results as model values, never as measurements.

## How it works, in four stages
- Stage 1 (Airy Stress Function & Negative-Definite Hessian): In-plane membrane stresses are written through an Airy stress function Φ(x, y) as σ_xx = Φ_yy, σ_yy = Φ_xx, σ_xy = −Φ_xy. In his 1870 paper on reciprocal figures Maxwell read a polyhedron lifted over a frame as a discrete stress function of this kind.
- Stage 2 (Rankine–Minkowski Reciprocal Polyhedra Γ ↔ Γ^*): In 3D polyhedral graphic statics (Akbarzadeh, Van Mele & Block 2015, DOI 10.1016/j.cad.2015.01.006), each member of the form diagram is perpendicular to a face of a closed cell in the force diagram, and the face area is proportional to the member force; a closed cell means the forces at that node balance.
- Stage 3 (Concavity and Compression): If the Hessian of Φ is negative-definite (Φ_xx < 0, Φ_yy < 0, det(H_Φ) = Φ_xx Φ_yy − Φ_xy² > 0), the in-plane stress field of the model is compressive everywhere. That is a property of the model under the load case considered, not a structural check of a real shell; the 52 mm thickness is an illustrative value.
- Stage 4 (8–13 μm Radiative Sky-Cooling Extrados & Toolbox X.03 CAD Bridge): With a porous P(VdF-HFP)-type coating (Mandal et al. 2018, DOI 10.1126/science.aat9513) assumed at R_solar = 0.96 under 890 W/m² of sun, the extrados absorbs 35.6 W/m². The model assigns a net radiative exchange with the sky of 134.0 W/m² at air temperature (a fixed illustrative value), so q_net = 134.0 − 35.6 = 98.4 W/m²; divided by an assumed combined heat-exchange coefficient of 13.9 W/(m²·K), that gives ΔT_eq ≈ −7.08 °C. Toolbox X.03 provides the STEP AP242 voussoir geometry.
