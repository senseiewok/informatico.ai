Act I — Wilhelm Nusselt, Kays & London's NTU Method, and Counter-Current Enthalpy Recovery

In 1915 the German engineer Wilhelm Nusselt published a paper that applied similarity (dimensionless) analysis to convective heat transfer; the Nusselt number is named after him, and the same kind of analysis was later carried over to mass transfer. In 1955 William Kays and Alexander London of Stanford University published the first edition of Compact Heat Exchangers, a book of design data that made the effectiveness–NTU method a standard tool. That method shows a simple fact: with two streams of equal heat capacity, a parallel-flow exchanger can never recover more than half of the available heat, while a counter-flow exchanger approaches full recovery as it is made larger.

In Plain Words · Extra-Easy Explanation

Imagine two long trains traveling on adjacent tracks in opposite directions during a freezing winter day. One train is warm and bustling with passengers, while the other train is cold and empty. If passengers on the warm train hand thermoses of hot coffee across the windows to the cold train as they pass, a wonderful exchange happens. Because the two trains move past each other from head to tail, the coldest incoming cars meet the coolest departing cars first, while the warmest cars meet at the very end. By the time they separate, the cold train has gathered almost all the warmth and humidity from the departing train. Modern buildings use this counter-flow trick inside their ventilation systems. Stale, warm indoor air leaving the building warms and humidifies fresh, chilly outdoor air entering the rooms through thin membrane sheets. The two air streams never touch, yet heat and fresh moisture glide right through.

Noses do something similar: the nasal lining warms and humidifies inhaled air and takes back some heat and water on the way out, though by alternating flow rather than two separate streams. Building energy recovery ventilators use vapor-permeable polymer membranes to exchange heat and moisture between outgoing and incoming air. Building upon William Kays and Alexander London's Effectiveness–NTU formalism (Compact Heat Exchangers, DOI DOI 10.1115/1.3644004 [Scite ↗]) and Wilhelm Nusselt's fully developed laminar duct heat transfer asymptotes, contemporary enthalpy recovery cores interleave microscopic hygroscopic membranes that simultaneously transfer sensible heat and latent water-vapor enthalpy. In these dense membrane blocks, exhaust air flows opposite to incoming outdoor air across sub-micron channels. Sensible energy conducts across the polymer skin while moisture dissolves into hydrophilic domains and diffuses down the vapor pressure gradient. Dense membranes are meant to keep the two air streams apart; how much air, odour or contaminant still crosses is a property of each product and has to be measured. Recovering heat and moisture this way can reduce heating and cooling loads.

Designing an energy recovery ventilator with sensible-only equations misses what happens to water. In freezing weather, warm exhaust air can cool below its dew point inside the core, and the condensate can freeze and block channels, which is why exchangers need defrost strategies. Moisture-permeable membrane cores move some of that water to the incoming air before it condenses, which the literature reports can reduce condensation and frost compared with plain plate exchangers (for example Van Vu & Dvořák 2017, DOI DOI 10.1051/epjconf/201714302080 [Scite ↗]). In humid weather, the coupled moisture transfer also matters for indoor humidity. Engineers handle these cases by combining effectiveness–NTU models with membrane sorption data; this page shows a simplified version.

Act II — How It Actually Works: Sensible Conduction + Solution-Diffusion Vapor Transport

Everyday Analogy & Interactive Controls Primer

Everyday Analogy: Think of two commuter trains passing each other in opposite directions on a snowy afternoon. If passengers on the heated departing train reach across the windows to hand warm mugs of tea to passengers on the incoming chilly train, heat transfers along the entire length of the tracks. The arriving passengers step off nearly as warm as the ones leaving, all without a single passenger ever switching train cars or mixing their personal belongings.

How the Two Sliders Work in Plain Words: To see how this energy recovery core functions in practice, our model features two primary adjustments that govern the transfer process. The first adjustment is the Sensible Transfer Units dial, which represents the effective surface area and thermal conductivity available for swapping dry heat. Turning this dial up is like lengthening the train platforms and adding more passenger windows, allowing thermal energy to glide effortlessly across the thin membrane dividers until the two streams nearly match temperatures. The second adjustment is the Membrane Moisture NTU dial, which represents the chemical capacity of the specialized membrane to absorb and transport water vapor. Raising this moisture control allows humidity to dissolve into microscopic pores and diffuse across the barrier into the dry supply stream. Balancing both dials ensures that fresh incoming air receives both the warmth and the gentle humidity needed for healthy indoor spaces, without letting liquid water freeze into damaging ice.

In a balanced counter-current membrane enthalpy exchanger, warm humid exhaust air flows opposite to cold dry outdoor air across dense, non-porous hydrophilic block-copolymer membranes. Sensible heat crosses the thin plastic boundary by pure thermal conduction, governed by the sensible transfer number NTU_s = U A / C_min. Simultaneously, water vapor molecules dissolve into sulfonated nano-domains and diffuse down chemical potential gradients toward the dry supply stream, governed by the latent transfer number NTU_m. Because counter-current geometry maintains a nearly constant thermodynamic driving gradient along the entire channel length L, its effectiveness epsilon = NTU / (1 + NTU) under balanced heat capacity rates (C_r = 1) asymptotically approaches unity as surface area expands. In contrast, parallel-flow exchangers remain thermodynamically constrained below fifty percent efficiency regardless of membrane size. Channel shape and membrane sorptivity set how many transfer units a real core reaches; in this model, the default NTU = 7.5 gives 88.2% sensible effectiveness. Real cores report lower or higher figures depending on airflow and design, and their frost behaviour must be tested. Live parameter coupling: NTU = 7.5 under governing law .

The coupled transfer of sensible heat and water vapor involves intricate conjugate boundary layer mechanics on either side of the selective membrane. Within the narrow flow channels, developing velocity and thermal boundary layers determine local convective resistance according to Graetz–Nusselt solutions. As moisture permeates the membrane via solution-diffusion, it releases or absorbs latent heat of vaporization, dynamically shifting the local membrane temperature. This thermal-latent coupling creates a localized feedback loop: higher temperature increases the saturation vapor pressure at the membrane interface, which modifies the local mass transfer driving force. Furthermore, if outdoor temperatures plunge below freezing, the exhaust channel wall can drop below the dew point and the freezing point simultaneously. If the local moisture removal rate governed by NTU_m is too slow to desorb water vapor before this freezing line, liquid condensate forms and crystallizes into frost. Advanced multi-scale modeling resolves these conjugate gradients, allowing engineers to tailor membrane thickness and channel spacing to prevent frost inception.
