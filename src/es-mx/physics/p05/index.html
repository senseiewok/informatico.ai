Act I — Gamow's Alpha Decay, WKB Wavefunction Tunneling, and Proton Transfer in Deaminase Enzymes

In the summer of 1928 at the University of Göttingen, the young Ukrainian theoretical physicist George Gamow cracked a foundational paradox of nuclear physics that had completely baffled Ernest Rutherford's laboratory. Experiments showed that alpha particles emitted from radioactive uranium possessed barely four million electron volts of kinetic energy, yet the repulsive electric barrier surrounding the nucleus was calculated at over twenty-five million electron volts. Under classical mechanics, an escaping particle climbing that mountain was an absolute physical impossibility, like a wooden ball rolling uphill over a mountain peak without being pushed. Drawing upon the new wave mechanics of Erwin Schrödinger and the semiclassical approximations of Wentzel, Kramers, and Brillouin, Gamow demonstrated that matter behaves as a wave packet that can bleed directly through a forbidden energy barrier. Inside enzyme active sites, identical quantum tunneling allows lightweight subatomic particles to pass directly through reaction barriers at body temperature.

For over a century, classical chemical kinetics operated under the Arrhenius framework and Eyring transition state theory, which assume that reacting atoms behave as point-like classical billiard balls that must gather thermal kinetic energy to climb over an activation barrier. If a reaction barrier is sixteen kilocalories per mole, classical physics predicts a reaction rate of mere seconds or minutes at body temperature. If one ignores quantum tunneling and models proton transfer purely as an over-the-barrier thermal climb, predicted catalytic rates are nearly an order of magnitude too slow, and the model completely misses the massive kinetic isotope effect observed when replacing hydrogen with deuterium.
