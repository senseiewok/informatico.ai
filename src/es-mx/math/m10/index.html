In 1895, French polymath Henri Poincaré published Analysis Situs, laying the bedrock of modern algebraic topology by asking what properties of a geometric object survive when it is stretched, twisted, or crumpled like a sheet of rubber. Poincaré understood that while metric distances and exact angles change under deformation, fundamental topological traits—such as whether a shape is a single connected piece, hollow like a basketball, or pierced by a tunnel like a donut—remain stubbornly invariant. Yet for more than a century, algebraic topology was considered too abstract for messy real-world data. If scientists gathered a three-dimensional cloud of laser scanner points from a porous rock or a delicate biological matrix, choosing how closely to connect the dots was an impossible guessing game. In 2002, computer scientists Herbert Edelsbrunner, David Letscher, and Afra Zomorodian revolutionized the field by showing that one should never choose a single threshold. Instead, by watching shapes form across expanding scales, true structures emerge as persistent musical bars.
