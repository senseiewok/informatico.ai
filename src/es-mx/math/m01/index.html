En Palabras Sencillas (Explicación Accesible): En palabras sencillas, el Último Teorema de Fermat plantea una pregunta muy clara sobre cómo encajan las piezas en nuestro universo: ¿es posible combinar dos potencias perfectas para formar una tercera de la misma dimensión? Con cuadrados planos es sencillo, como cuando colocamos losetas en el piso de la cocina siguiendo el clásico triángulo de lados tres, cuatro y cinco. No obstante, al intentar apilar bloques tridimensionales para formar un cubo perfecto más grande, la geometría rígida deja huecos inevitables. Pierre de Fermat escribió en 1637 que esto era imposible para cualquier potencia mayor que dos, pero dejó al mundo sin su demostración. Lo fascinante es que este misterio no era una simple curiosidad aritmética. Para resolverlo, los matemáticos tuvieron que construir puentes entre universos que parecían no tener relación: las curvas elípticas y las formas modulares. Uno de esos objetos, la curva elíptica, también se usa en una familia muy extendida de criptografía de clave pública. Es un objeto compartido, no una consecuencia: la demostración del teorema de Fermat no vuelve seguro ningún cifrado, y la seguridad de la criptografía de curvas elípticas depende de supuestos distintos que siguen sin demostrarse.

Mecanismo Técnico, Fuerzas en Competencia y Umbral Crítico: El núcleo matemático de esta demostración se comprende al observar la interacción entre dos parámetros fundamentales: el exponente geométrico y la densidad de restricciones del sistema. En el deslizador del exponente, el valor dos permite que los enteros se acomoden en perfecta simetría pitagórica; pero en cuanto el exponente sube a tres o más, la deformación de la curva elíptica de Frey genera un discriminante imposible. Por su parte, la densidad de restricciones determina si las ramas lógicas encuentran una salida o chocan contra una contradicción total. El obstáculo histórico consistía en que comparar directamente el anillo de deformaciones con el álgebra de Hecke era demasiado difícil; este folio lo dibuja como una barrera alta. Andrew Wiles y Richard Taylor resolvieron el bloqueo introduciendo primos auxiliares que actúan como un andamio temporal; el folio lo dibuja como una barrera más baja. Los dos valores de barrera, 8.39 y 3.59, son valores ilustrativos del modelo y no aparecen en ningún artículo. Una vez que las piezas embonan en el límite, el andamio se retira y la forma cuspidal buscada tendría que vivir en un espacio de dimensión cero. Act I — The Human Story & Historical Turning Point (M.01)

Around 1637 (the date is traditional rather than documented) the French lawyer and mathematician Pierre de Fermat wrote a note in the margin of his copy of the Arithmetica of Diophantus, in Claude Bachet's Latin edition. Beside the eighth problem of the second book, which asks for a square to be divided into two squares, he remarked that a cube cannot be split into two cubes, nor a fourth power into two fourth powers, nor in general any power above the second into two like powers. He added that he had found a truly marvellous proof which the margin was too narrow to contain. The note became public only after his death in 1665, when his son Samuel printed an edition of the Arithmetica with his father's annotations in 1670. No general proof by Fermat has ever been found. The statement then resisted every attempt for more than three and a half centuries.

The historical impasse broke only when modern mathematicians stopped treating integer equations as isolated puzzles and linked them to two profound geometric realms: elliptic curves and modular forms. In the mid-1980s Gerhard Frey observed that a hypothetical counterexample would give a semistable elliptic curve so unusual that it should not be modular. After Ken Ribet established the level-lowering statement known as the epsilon conjecture in 1986, Andrew Wiles worked for about seven years, largely in private, on the Taniyama–Shimura modularity conjecture for semistable elliptic curves. A gap was found in 1993; Wiles, working with Richard Taylor, repaired it in September 1994, and the two papers appeared in 1995 (Annals of Mathematics, DOI DOI 10.2307/2118559 [Scite ↗] and DOI DOI 10.2307/2118560 [Scite ↗]). In 2026 a public repository, github.com/anthropics/fermats-last-theorem, published Lean 4 sources that its README describes as a complete, machine-checked proof of the theorem, with a page for each of 29,511 theorems, and with portions derived from the Imperial College London formalisation project led by Kevin Buzzard (github.com/ImperialCollegeLondon/FLT). Those are the repository's own statements. This site has not built or checked the proof.

Turning a proof of more than a hundred pages into something a computer can check matters for a plain reason. Mathematical consensus has long depended on human refereeing, and subtle missteps can hide inside dense calculations. Wiles's first announced argument had such a gap, in its use of an Euler system, and closing it took about a year. A proof assistant such as Lean 4 replaces part of that trust with a mechanical check: every definition and lemma has to be accepted by a small kernel, and no step can be skipped. What a kernel cannot check is whether the formal statement means what a reader thinks it means, or whether an intermediate theorem deserves its name. The README of the Fermat repository says as much, and that reading is still a human job. For this page it also means one more thing: a claim that a proof is machine-checked is a claim about a build that someone has to run, and this site has not run it.

Act II — How It Actually Works: Plain-Language Intuition to Governing Law (M.01)

Analogía Cotidiana y Guía de Controles

Everyday Analogy: Imagine building a sweeping stone arch bridge across a turbulent mountain gorge. If you attempt to hoist a single massive stone beam across the gap, the material fractures under its own bending weight. However, by erecting temporary wooden scaffold towers in the riverbed, builders support intermediate blocks and relieve shear stress until the final keystone locks into place. Once the arch achieves self-supporting geometric equilibrium, the temporary scaffolding can be dismantled completely, leaving an enduring bridge.

How the Two Sliders Work in Plain Words: Before looking at any equations, you can understand how this mathematical bridge operates through two intuitive control knobs. The first knob controls the geometric building block, shifting from flat squares to rigid three-dimensional cubes and higher dimensions. When this exponent knob rests at two, numbers fit together like flat square tiles on a bathroom floor, making balanced combinations easy to find. But as soon as you turn the knob to three or higher, the geometry stiffens, making it impossible for two integer blocks to combine into a third without leaving cracks. The second knob measures congestion, tracking how many overlapping rules or constraints crowd into the workspace. When rules are sparse, candidate shapes can flex and wiggle freely. ## Recent research context
- **A Public Lean 4 Repository States a Complete Formal Proof of Fermat's Last Theorem**: Andrew Wiles's proof appeared in the Annals of Mathematics in 1995 (DOI 10.2307/2118559), with the companion paper by Taylor and Wiles (DOI 10.2307/2118560). The repository https://github.com/anthropics/fermats-last-theorem states that it holds a complete machine-checked Lean 4 proof following Frey, Serre, Ribet, Wiles and Taylor-Wiles, with a page for each of 29,511 theorems, built in part on the Imperial College London project led by Kevin Buzzard. This site reports that statement. It has not built the repository or checked the proof.
