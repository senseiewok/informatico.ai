Act I — From Lev Pontryagin's Maximum Principle to 2026 Formula 1 350 kW Hybrid Control & Neural PDE Operators

Lev Semenovich Pontryagin, blind from an accident at the age of fourteen, led a group at the Steklov Mathematical Institute in Moscow with Vladimir Boltyanskii, Revaz Gamkrelidze and Evgenii Mishchenko. Their maximum principle, first announced in the mid-1950s and set out in their 1962 book The Mathematical Theory of Optimal Processes, handles a problem the classical calculus of variations of Euler and Lagrange did not: controls that have hard limits. Real engines face such limits: valves hit stops, motors overheat, and batteries drain. The principle attaches to each state a shadow price, the co-state, which tracks the future value of a unit of that resource and tells the controller when to use full power, when to hold back, and when to brake.

Optimal control has been applied to hybrid race cars for more than a decade. In 2014 Limebeer, Perantoni and Rao published an optimal-control study of the energy recovery systems of a Formula One car (International Journal of Control, DOI DOI 10.1080/00207179.2014.900705 [Scite ↗]), written for the rules of that era, when the kinetic motor-generator was limited to 120 kilowatts. The 2026 rules, as widely reported, raise that limit to 350 kilowatts; this folio has not checked the regulation text, and its 4.0 megajoule per-lap energy budget is a model assumption. In the model, if the motor deploys full boost too early on a straight, the battery reaches its limit before the braking zone and the car loses time. Computing drag and downforce for many wing angles and ride heights with conventional flow solvers is slow. The Fourier Neural Operator (Li et al., arXiv:2010.08895) learns the solution operator of a PDE by parameterising an integral kernel in Fourier space; its authors report speedups of up to three orders of magnitude over conventional solvers on their benchmark equations. Using such a surrogate inside a Pontryagin controller for a race car is this folio's proposal, not something shown here.

The stakes of this mathematical convergence extend far beyond qualifying laps and checkered flags. For decades, optimal control and partial differential equation modeling lived on opposite sides of a vast computational canyon. Classical control theorists knew how to write down exact optimality equations for simple spring-mass systems, but the moment a problem involved turbulent airflow, swirling thermal boundaries, or complex physical media, the equations became too heavy to evaluate in real time. Engineers had to rely on clumsy lookup tables, pre-baked approximations, or conservative safety margins that left dramatic amounts of performance on the table. Pairing Pontryagin's Hamiltonian formulation with learned neural operators is one proposed way to close that gap between continuum physics and fast decisions; it remains a research direction. The maximum principle on its own is long established, for example in spacecraft trajectory optimisation.

§ I.1 · Obstrucción Clásica y Frontera Estructural

Every enduring equation begins as a human question—an act of patient curiosity by investigators who refused to accept an opaque boundary as the final word. Tracing the intellectual arc of Folio M.07 from its earliest formulation in 1956 (Boltyanskii, Gamkrelidze & Pontryagin announce the maximum principle for optimal control with bounded controls (book with Mishchenko, 1962).) to its modern resolution in 2020–2021 (Li, Kovachki, Azizzadenesheli et al. introduce the Fourier Neural Operator for parametric PDEs (arXiv:2010.08895; ICLR 2021).) reveals why un-coupled classical approximations repeatedly stalled at a hard analytical barrier. Whenever investigators attempted to treat the governing relation H(x, u, λ) = 1/v + λ_v f_v(v, P_k, δ) − λ_E P_k/(η v) · u_θ = F⁻¹(R_φ · F(a)) as a purely local or linearized perturbation, higher-order coupling terms across the kernel triad (Pontryagin Maximum Principle (PMP) · HJB PDE · Fourier Neural Operator) amplified truncation error rather than damping it. What appeared for decades to be an intractable computational or experimental pathology was, in reality, a structural phase boundary embedded in the operator domain itself.

Resolving that impasse required shifting from ad-hoc empirical curve fitting to a rigorous, multi-stage operator formulation anchored in Model values: P_k,max = 350 kW · ΔE_lap = 4.0 MJ (assumed budget) · FNO error 0.82% (illustrative, not measured). Specifically, the system’s trajectory is governed by control parameter P_1 (MGU-K Peak Electric Power (P_k)) spanning the admissible interval [120.0, 350.0] with step resolution Δ = 10.0 and canonical reference operating point kW350.0; control parameter P_2 (Fourier Spectral Modes (k_max)) spanning the admissible interval [8, 64] with step resolution Δ = 4 and canonical reference operating point modes32. Within this state space, any valid proof or physical design must simultaneously satisfy local conservation laws, global boundary conditions, and strict asymptotic scaling—setting the stage for the four-chapter formal derivation in Act II.

Act II — How It Actually Works: Pontryagin Co-State Switching + Fourier Neural Operator Spectral Convolutions

Analogía Cotidiana y Guía de Controles

Everyday Analogy: Imagine running a grueling trail marathon through rolling mountains with only a single twenty-ounce water flask. If you drink greedily on the early flat path because you feel thirsty, your flask runs empty on the sunny ridge, leaving you dehydrated and staggering before the summit. But if you horde the water too stingily, you finish the race fatigued with ounces to spare. Pontryagin's shadow price acts like an internal coach, calculating the exact value of each sip so you hydrate precisely on steep ascents where energy yields the greatest speed.

How the Two Sliders Work in Plain Words: To see how this works in practice, imagine having two interactive dials on the dashboard of your racing simulator. The first dial governs the maximum electrical punch delivered by the hybrid motor, scaling from a conservative 120 kilowatts up to a thunderous 350 kilowatts. When you dial this power up, the car launches down the asphalt with astonishing thrust, but your energy reservoir drains at nearly triple the speed, threatening to cut power abruptly before the next corner. The second dial controls the visual and mathematical clarity of the airflow simulator, increasing the number of wave frequencies used to map the air rushing over the car from a blurry eight modes up to a razor-sharp sixty-four modes. With too few wave modes, the computer misjudges wind resistance and gives bad advice. But as you turn both dials in harmony, you watch the virtual coach find the exact sweet spot: delivering maximum electric acceleration off slow bends, then smoothly tapering off the boost at blistering speeds where aerodynamic drag makes further electric expenditure wasteful.

## How it works, in four stages
- Stage 1 (Fourier Neural Operator Surrogate, proposed): An FNO layer u_{t+1} = σ(W u_t + F⁻¹(R_φ · F u_t)) could be trained to map wing angle δ_wing and ride height h_ride to drag and downforce coefficients. No such surrogate was trained for this folio.
- Stage 2 (Distance-Domain Pontryagin Hamiltonian H): Formulates minimum-time lap control with ds/dt = v. While the battery stays inside its limits, the battery co-state λ_E* is constant; the folio calibrates it to an assumed 4.0 MJ-per-lap energy budget.
- Stage 3 (Bang–Singular–Bang Taper vs. Clipping): Above a switching speed the model tapers P_k*(s) instead of deploying full power until the battery clips. The 298 km/h switching speed and the 0.42 s saving are illustrative model values.
- Stage 4 (Regenerative Braking Harvest): Under braking the MGU-K recovers energy so that the periodic boundary condition E_batt(L) = E_batt(0) closes the lap.
