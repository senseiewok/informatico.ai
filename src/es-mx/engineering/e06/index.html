Act I — From Maurice Biot's Response Spectrum to William Robinson's Lead-Rubber Seismic Isolator

In the spring of 1932, amidst the wreckage of destructive California and Tokyo earthquakes, the young Belgian-American engineer Maurice Anthony Biot sat in his Pasadena office at Caltech. Troubled by how unpredictably buildings responded to shaking ground, Biot formulated the earthquake response spectrum: a master mathematical curve revealing how different structures amplify ground motion depending on their natural swaying tempo. Four decades later, researchers showed that buildings could survive violent shaking by bending into the inelastic range, yet this internal damage ruined piping and halted operations. In 1974, inside an industrial workshop in Lower Hutt, New Zealand, the physicist William Robinson solved this fundamental dilemma. Instead of building stiffer walls to fight the earth, Robinson vulcanized flexible rubber layers around a solid central lead plug. Placed beneath a building, his isolator decoupled the structure from ground movements, absorbing seismic shocks through plastic deformation while the building above glided smoothly.

The nonlinear response of lead-rubber bearings involves intimate coupling between mechanical kinematics and thermodynamics. During cyclic lateral displacement, alternating elastomeric layers provide high vertical stiffness to support building gravity loads while maintaining low horizontal shear stiffness. Within the central cylindrical cavity, the solid lead plug undergoes severe plastic shear strain exceeding one hundred percent. Because lead recrystallizes dynamically at ambient temperatures, it can endure hundreds of large-amplitude cyclic reversals without work-hardening or brittle fatigue failure. However, the plastic work dissipated during severe ground shaking converts directly into internal heat. In extreme ground motions, internal lead temperatures can rise by over fifty degrees Celsius, causing temporary thermal softening that slightly reduces characteristic yield strength Q_d. Sophisticated nonlinear response history models account for this hysteretic heat generation alongside P-Delta overturning moments, verifying that restoring forces in the vulcanized rubber ensure reliable post-earthquake recentering and prevent permanent structural offsets even after multiple seismic aftershocks.
