Act I — Continuing A.04 Exoskeletons, A.03 Daylighting & M.05 Rigidity: The Mathematics of Origami Building Envelopes

Koryo Miura, a Japanese engineer at the Institute of Space and Astronautical Science, worked on ways to pack large membranes, such as solar arrays, for launch and open them in space. His 1985 report describes a fold of identical parallelograms set in a herringbone pattern. A sheet folded this way opens and closes in a single motion: pull two opposite corners and it expands in length and width together. The pattern is now called Miura-ori. In 2013 Mark Schenk and Simon Guest at the University of Cambridge analysed the Miura fold as a mechanical metamaterial and showed, among other results, that its in-plane Poisson ratio is negative (PNAS, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1217998110). This folio is a design study of what that geometry could mean for sun-shading facades.

Miura-ori was developed by Koryo Miura for packaging and deploying large membranes in space (ISAS Report 618, 1985). In 2013 Mark Schenk and Simon D. Guest published an analysis in PNAS (DOI DOI 10.1073/pnas.1217998110 [Scite ↗]) that treats the Miura-ori sheet as a mechanical metamaterial and shows that its in-plane Poisson ratio is negative. Its degree-four vertices satisfy Kawasaki's flat-foldability condition, and spherical trigonometry links the fold angles of the creases at each vertex; this folio uses the relation tan(gamma_2 / 2) = cos(alpha) * tan(gamma_1 / 2) and writes the in-plane Poisson ratio as nu_xy = -tan^2(phi) with its own simplified expression for phi. In built architecture, the Al Bahar Towers in Abu Dhabi carry origami-like, umbrella-shaped shading units covered in PTFE-coated fiber mesh; their designers report that the screen cuts the facade's solar heat gain (g-value) by more than half compared with bare glazing (Karanouh & Kerber 2015, DOI DOI 10.3233/fde-150040 [Scite ↗]). Applying rigid Miura origami to a solar screen (specified under CSI 10 71 13, Exterior Sun Control Devices) is the design idea this folio explores.

Builders in hot regions, from Cairo to Isfahan and Damascus, long used carved wooden lattice screens known as mashrabiya to temper sun and glare and to give privacy. In 1987 Jean Nouvel's Institut du Monde Arabe in Paris reinterpreted the mashrabiya as a wall of mechanical, light-sensitive apertures. Mechanical facades like that rely on many moving parts that need upkeep. The idea explored here is to put the motion into the geometry of the folds, so a whole screen could be moved by one actuator. That is a design hypothesis: real hinges have play and friction, and a real origami facade could still bind or wear.

Act II — How It Actually Works: Spherical Degree-4 Vertex Kinematics + Auxetic Poisson Inversion

Analogía Cotidiana y Guía de Controles

Everyday Analogy: Think of an old-fashioned paper road map that has been folded with accordion creases: when you try to pull it open carelessly, the paper snags, binds, and tears along the folds. Now imagine a fold pattern where every crease works in harmony, like an expanding concertina or a pop-up paper umbrella: gently pulling two opposite corners causes every folded facet to open simultaneously without twisting or jamming. That synchronized unfolding is the core of rigid Miura origami.

How the Two Sliders Work in Plain Words: To control an origami facade on a skyscraper, you only need to adjust two geometric knobs. The first knob controls the deployment fold angle, which measures how far the folded panels have opened or closed. When this knob is set to a low angle, the panels fold flat against each other, projecting outward like an array of open umbrellas that block direct desert sun from entering the building. As the afternoon cools and the sun sinks toward the horizon, dialing this knob open flattens the panels back against the glass wall, admitting panoramic city views and gentle evening light. The second knob sets the sector angle of the individual parallelogram tiles that make up the origami pattern. If you choose a steep, narrow diamond tile, the facade opens with deep three-dimensional relief, creating dramatic sculptural shadows. If you choose a wider angle, the panels move more gently, acting like a lightweight, low-profile solar filter across the glass curtain wall.

Continuing the Maxwell–Calladine kinematic mechanism count of Folio A.04 (m = 1 single collective mechanism) and the distance geometry theorems of Folio M.05, consider a Miura-ori unit cell composed of four rigid planar parallelogram facets with acute sector angle alpha (typically 55° to 78°) meeting at an interior degree-four vertex. When actuated by the primary fold angle theta in the range from 10° to 80°, this folio uses a simplified parametrization of its own for the semi-width angle, cos(phi) = sin(alpha) * cos(theta / 2), and couples the secondary fold angle gamma_2 to the primary fold angle gamma_1 (taken equal to theta) via tan(gamma_2 / 2) = cos(alpha) * tan(gamma_1 / 2). It then writes the in-plane Poisson ratio as nu_xy = -tan^2(phi) < 0, the form Schenk & Guest give for their own fold-angle definition; with the folio's parametrization the default settings give phi ≈ 31.3° and nu_xy ≈ -0.37, and gamma_2 ≈ 16.2°.
