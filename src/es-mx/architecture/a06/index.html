Mecanismo Técnico, Fuerzas en Competencia y Umbral Crítico: La ingeniería hidrosanitaria sustentable opera bajo un esquema biológico dual: biorremediación ecológica en el exterior y control térmico y por luz ultravioleta en el interior (metas de diseño). En el humedal subsuperficial, las aguas grises fluyen bajo una capa seca de grava volcánica donde, en el modelo del folio, la rizosfera reduce alrededor del noventa y cinco por ciento de la DBO_5 (con C_ent = 140 mg/L y los valores supuestos del folio C* = 3.0 mg/L y k = 1.12 por día, C_sal = 3.0 + 137 × e^(−1.12 × 3.2) = 6.8 mg/L); mantener el agua bajo la grava busca limitar el contacto con la superficie. En las redes interiores de agua potable, las metas de diseño del folio son la recirculación continua a 60 °C o más, sin ramales estancados. Complementariamente, un reactor ultravioleta en línea de 254 nanómetros aplica una dosis germicida que, en el modelo del folio, equivale a 5.4 reducciones logarítmicas; es un valor de diseño, no una medición. Act I — From Roman Cisterns to Net-Zero Bio-Engineered Wetlands & Hot-Water Recirculation Design

The German botanist Käthe Seidel began experiments with wetland plants for wastewater treatment in the 1950s (see Vymazal 2005, DOI 10.1016/j.ecoleng.2005.07.010, for this history). The idea was that common wetland plants, and the microbes around their roots, could do part of the work of a treatment plant. Later designers ran wastewater horizontally through gravel beds planted with reeds such as Phragmites australis, keeping the water below a dry surface. Recycling water inside a building raises a second problem, which this folio takes up: how to encourage living biology outside while keeping microbial growth in the indoor plumbing under control.

In high-performance regenerative architecture, water management must resolve the tension between ecological closed-loop conservation and stringent microbiological safety. In their foundational monograph Treatment Wetlands (DOI DOI 10.1201/9781420012514 [Scite ↗]), Robert Kadlec and Scott Wallace describe the first-order k-C* model used to size subsurface constructed wetlands. In this folio's worked example that model gives a 95.1 percent reduction in BOD_5; real beds vary with loading, temperature and age. Building water systems can also harbor biofilms of opportunistic organisms. ASHRAE Standard 188, Legionellosis: Risk Management for Building Water Systems, addresses Legionella risk; it does not prescribe this folio's design. The design here strictly isolates exterior non-potable greywater recycling from interior potable distribution networks. Open tools such as DedalusProject/dedalus (github.com/DedalusProject/dedalus, DOI DOI 10.1103/PhysRevResearch.2.023068 [Scite ↗]) for transport equations and dynamicslab/pysindy (DOI DOI 10.1073/pnas.1517384113 [Scite ↗]) for fitting rate laws could be used to study such a system; this folio does not run them. Its recirculating thermal loop and 254-nanometer UV-C reactor are design targets meant to limit biofilm growth near the point of use, not a tested installation.

From Roman aqueducts to nineteenth-century municipal water grids, urban architecture operated on an unsustainable linear throughput model: extract potable water from distant reservoirs, use it once, and dump municipal effluent into coastal waterways. Water-saving measures such as low-flow plumbing fixtures and reclaimed greywater cisterns reduce demand on municipal supply. Conservation by rule of thumb can create a new risk. Reducing flow rates in oversized pipes can leave stagnant dead-legs where water sits lukewarm, between thirty and forty degrees Celsius. Under these conditions, opportunistic waterborne bacteria can colonize pipe walls and form biofilms that are harder to disinfect. Droplets from showerheads and sink drains are one route by which such organisms can reach people. Realizing true net-zero water requires mastering both ecological wetland biology outside and clinical thermal disinfection inside.

§ I.1 · Obstrucción Clásica y Frontera Estructural

Every enduring equation begins as a human question—an act of patient curiosity by investigators who refused to accept an opaque boundary as the final word. Tracing the intellectual arc of Folio A.06 from its earliest formulation in 1950s–1960s (Käthe Seidel experiments with wetland plants for wastewater treatment in Germany.) to its modern resolution in 2017 (Kotay et al. use a fluorescent marker organism to model bacterial dispersion from hand-washing sink traps (DOI DOI 10.1128/AEM.03327-16 [Scite ↗]).) reveals why un-coupled classical approximations repeatedly stalled at a hard analytical barrier. Whenever investigators attempted to treat the governing relation C_out = C* + (C_in−C*)e^(−kτ) · 5.4-Log UV-C as a purely local or linearized perturbation, higher-order coupling terms across the kernel triad (Kadlec-Knight k-C* · Chick-Watson 5.4-Log UV-C) amplified truncation error rather than damping it. What appeared for decades to be an intractable computational or experimental pathology was, in reality, a structural phase boundary embedded in the operator domain itself.

Resolving that impasse required shifting from ad-hoc empirical curve fitting to a rigorous, multi-stage operator formulation anchored in Model values: 95.1% BOD_5 removal · 5.4-log UV-C design dose. Specifically, the system’s trajectory is governed by control parameter P_1 (Wetland Hydraulic Retention Time (τ_HRT)) spanning the admissible interval [0.8, 6.0] with step resolution Δ = 0.2 and canonical reference operating point d3.2; control parameter P_2 (254 nm UV-C Reactor Fluence (H_UV)) spanning the admissible interval [10.0, 80.0] with step resolution Δ = 1.0 and canonical reference operating point mJ/cm²45.0. Within this state space, any valid proof or physical design must simultaneously satisfy local conservation laws, global boundary conditions, and strict asymptotic scaling—setting the stage for the four-chapter formal derivation in Act II.

Act II — How It Actually Works: Kadlec–Knight Darcy Beds + Chick–Watson UV-C Fluence

Analogía Cotidiana y Guía de Controles

Everyday Analogy: Think of the difference between a swampy puddle and a high-alpine brook. A warm puddle of rainwater sitting on a garden hose turns murky and slimy within days. In contrast, a tumbling mountain stream flowing rapidly over clean granite gravel under intense alpine sunshine stays sparkling, oxygenated, and clean. Bio-engineered plumbing aims to imitate that mountain stream: subsurface gravel beds naturally filter outdoor greywater, while continuous hot circulation and ultraviolet light help keep indoor pipes clean.

How the Two Sliders Work in Plain Words: In the interactive laboratory below, you calibrate the dual biological barriers of a net-zero water building. The first slider sets the hydraulic retention time of the outdoor gravel wetland bed, measuring how many days greywater spends percolating through the reed root systems. Extending this retention time gives the beneficial microbes living in the gravel more time to consume soaps and organic nutrients, yielding clean, polished water ready for toilet flushing and garden irrigation. The second slider controls the germicidal ultraviolet radiation dose delivered inside the indoor potable water reactor. As you turn up this ultraviolet fluence dial, ultraviolet light damages bacterial genetic material and keeps more of the organisms from reproducing. By balancing adequate natural wetland residence time with an adequate ultraviolet dose, the model building recycles water while meeting its two design targets. The readouts are model outputs, not measurements. They are not a safety threshold and not infection-control advice.

The dual-boundary engineering of sustainable building water systems couples outdoor ecological bioremediation with indoor photochemical disinfection. Outside the envelope, subsurface gravel wetlands follow the first-order Kadlec-Knight plug-flow model, C_out = C* + (C_in - C*) * exp(-k_v * tau_HRT), as described in Kadlec and Wallace (Treatment Wetlands, 2nd ed., DOI DOI 10.1201/9781420012514 [Scite ↗]). Operating beneath a dry basalt gravel mulch cap avoids an open water surface while Phragmites root zones supply oxygen to micro-aerobic biofilms, giving 95.1% BOD_5 removal across a 3.2-day retention time in the model (with C_in = 140 mg/L and the folio's assumed C* = 3.0 mg/L and k = 1.12 per day, C_out = 3.0 + 137 × e^(−1.12 × 3.2) = 6.8 mg/L). Inside the potable loop, ASHRAE Standard 188 addresses Legionella risk management in building water systems; it does not prescribe this design. The folio's own targets are continuous recirculation at or above 60 degrees Celsius and 0.6 meters per second, with no stagnant dead-legs. Downstream, an inline annular 254-nanometer UV-C photoreactor delivers a germicidal fluence of H = 45 millijoules per square centimeter, which the folio's Chick-Watson model converts to a 5.4-log reduction (0.12 log per mJ/cm²). That rate constant is a design assumption. Hijnen et al. (2006, DOI DOI 10.1016/j.watres.2005.10.030 [Scite ↗]) review UV inactivation credit across viruses, bacteria and protozoa; the folio's own inference is that a single constant cannot represent every organism. Nothing here was simulated in DedalusProject/dedalus (github.com/DedalusProject/dedalus, DOI DOI 10.1103/PhysRevResearch.2.023068 [Scite ↗]) or dynamicslab/pysindy (DOI DOI 10.1073/pnas.1517384113 [Scite ↗]); they are named as tools a reader could use to test the design. Live parameter coupling: Wetland Hydraulic Retention Time = 3.2 d under governing law .

The wetland and the potable loop work at different scales and never share water. In the subsurface reed bed, Darcy's law Q = K_s * A_c * S sets the horizontal seepage through basalt gravel. If the hydraulic retention time is cut too short by overloading the bed with greywater, organic matter passes through the root zone unoxidized and the outlet turns more turbid. That matters for reuse: if greywater were later disinfected with UV for non-potable uses, turbid effluent would scatter the light and need a higher dose. In this design the potable UV-C reactor never receives greywater; the two systems stay separate. At clinical sink basins, offset drains and self-heating P-traps are proposed to reduce drain droplets (untested).
