In the summer of 1827, Carl Friedrich Gauss worked through field notebooks at his observatory desk in Göttingen after years of surveying triangulation chains across the Kingdom of Hanover. Using brass theodolites, his field crews had measured tiny excesses in the corner angles of large triangles spanning hilltops from the Brocken to the Inselsberg. Gauss noticed a striking geometric fact. Even if a curved sheet bends like a saddle or a dome, the total curvature inside a closed patch depends solely on distances measured along the surface itself. Nearly two centuries later, engineers who slice smooth shells into flat digital triangles face the discrete cousin of that discovery. A curved canopy exported from a modeling tool can look clean on screen while narrow sliver triangles quietly invert stiffness weights. Auditing corner angles restores that physical compass.

Converting smooth curved surfaces into polyhedral meshes exposes a classic tension between topological invariants and metric discretization. Ulrich Pinkall and Konrad Polthier (Experimental Mathematics, DOI 10.1080/10586458.1993.10504266 [Scite ↗]) derived the cotangent formula for discrete minimal surfaces, expressing mean-curvature normals at each vertex via the cotangents of opposite interior angles. Soon after, Mathieu Desbrun, Mark Meyer, Peter Schröder, and Alan Barr (SIGGRAPH 1999, DOI 10.1145/311535.311576 [Scite ↗]) applied implicit curvature flow to fair noisy meshes without shrinking feature volume, and Meyer, Desbrun, Schröder, and Barr (Visualization and Mathematics III, DOI 10.1007/978-3-662-05105-4_2 [Scite ↗]) introduced mixed Voronoi cell areas to clamp obtuse triangles that push circumcenters outside their host face. When a stretched mesh violates the local Delaunay condition along even a handful of seams, discrete harmonic heat flow or membrane smoothing can produce spurious ripples that overshoot boundary temperatures or elevations. Conversely, checking interior angles with standard trigonometric identities takes less than fifty milliseconds on a desktop or laptop.
