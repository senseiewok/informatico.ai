Act I — de Gennes's Reptating Snake, Doi–Edwards Tube Theory, and Large-Cargo DNA/RNA Reptation

In 1971, Pierre-Gilles de Gennes asked a deceptively simple question: how does a long, flexible polymer chain move when it is hopelessly entangled inside a dense mesh of fixed obstacles that it cannot cross? Drawing on the Latin reptare (to creep like a snake), de Gennes showed that surrounding obstacles confine the chain of N monomers to a virtual topological tube of diameter a_tube. Unable to move sideways, the chain must slither back and forth along its own contour, taking a disengagement time tau_d ~ N^3 to escape its original tube and diffusing with center-of-mass diffusivity D_rep ~ N^{-2}.

In 1978, Masao Doi and Sir Sam Edwards turned de Gennes's reptation insight into the complete tube constitutive theory of entangled polymer viscoelasticity. A decade later, molecular biologists realized that reptation is the exact physical mechanism governing gel electrophoresis of kilobase DNA/mRNA and the transport of elongated filamentous nanocarriers and ribonucleoprotein complexes through crowded extracellular matrices and nuclear pores.

Act II — How It Actually Works: Rouse Modes vs. Doi–Edwards Tube Disengagement
