In sixteen seventy-five, Robert Hooke concealed his greatest discovery inside a Latin anagram: as hangs the flexible chain, so, but inverted, stands the rigid arch. For nearly three centuries, master masons carved cathedrals by intuition and geometric proportion, trusting that pure compression could conquer gravity without steel. Yet by the mid-twentieth century, modern structural engineering had largely abandoned unreinforced stone, treating masonry with elastic beam equations that falsely predicted catastrophic failure whenever tensile stress appeared. Standing before the soaring stone vaults of King’s College Chapel in Cambridge in nineteen sixty-six, structural historian Jacques Heyman recognized the profound error of this modern dogma. He realized that historic masonry does not fail by crushing or material yielding, but by geometric instability when the line of thrust wanders outside the masonry blocks. Today, Philippe Block at ETH Zurich transforms that insight into digital computation.

Heyman formalized his breakthrough in his seminal nineteen sixty-six treatise 'The Stone Skeleton' (DOI: DOI 10.1016/0020-7683(66)90018-7 [Scite ↗]), establishing plastic limit analysis for masonry. Under these assumptions, the Safe Theorem guarantees that if any admissible thrust line can be drawn wholly within the arch boundaries in equilibrium with applied loads, the structure cannot collapse. James Clerk Maxwell’s eighteen sixty-four reciprocal force polygons and William Rankine’s graphic statics provided the geometric machinery to trace these equilibrium lines by hand.
