Act I — Wilhelm Nusselt, Kays & London's NTU Method, and Counter-Current Enthalpy Recovery

During the damp winter of 1915 in Munich, the mechanical engineer Wilhelm Nusselt investigated how thermal energy and moisture travel across thin moving boundaries of air. By analyzing laminar flow adjacent to solid surfaces, Nusselt demonstrated that thermal conduction and vapor diffusion share the exact same underlying mathematical harmony. Four decades later, in sunny California, William Kays and Alexander London stood before rows of experimental finned coils at Stanford University. Seeking lighter, more efficient heat exchangers for modern aircraft and industrial buildings, they published their groundbreaking monograph on compact heat exchangers in 1955. Kays and London introduced a practical metric called the Number of Transfer Units, proving that arranging two fluid streams in opposing, counter-current directions shatters the fifty-percent efficiency barrier inherent in parallel flow, capturing almost all available thermal energy without requiring impossible amounts of machinery.

Conversely, during humid summer conditions, failing to model coupled moisture diffusion leads to chronic indoor over-humidification, fostering toxic mold blooms and degrading mass timber structural frames.
