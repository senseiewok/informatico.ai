Act I — Wallace Sabine's Midnight Cushions at Harvard and the Physics of Clear Speech

In the quiet autumn midnight of 1895, Wallace Clement Sabine, a young assistant professor of physics at Harvard University, slipped through the shadows of Harvard Yard carrying an armful of heavy velvet seat cushions. The university had just completed the new Fogg Lecture Hall, but the space was an acoustic catastrophe: when a lecturer uttered a single word, the sound bounced incessantly off hard plaster walls for over five seconds, turning sentences into an unintelligible acoustic roar. Handed the embarrassing problem by the university corporation, Sabine worked strictly between midnight and dawn, when horse-drawn streetcars stopped running and the city fell silent. Armed only with a brass organ pipe, a stopwatch, and hundreds of borrowed dining hall cushions, Sabine painstakingly measured how sound decayed across the dark hall, searching for the hidden mathematical order connecting architectural volume, surface texture, and the crystal clarity of the human voice.
