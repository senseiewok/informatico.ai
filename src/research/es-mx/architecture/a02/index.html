Act I — The Human Story & Historical Turning Point (A.02)

Along the bustling, silted banks of the Bian River in eleventh-century Kaifeng, master carpenters of the Song Dynasty faced an engineering crisis. How could they span a broad, turbulent waterway to let grain barges pass without planting timber piers into the churning riverbed, when no single log in the imperial forestry yards was long enough to reach across? Their solution was as daring as it was poetic: an interlocking weave of short, overlapping fir beams that mutually supported one another like woven silk threads. Four centuries later, in Milan, Leonardo da Vinci stood in his studio sketching the same self-supporting pinwheel in his notebook, marvelling at how short wooden poles could cross wide ravines without rope or iron nails. Each beam rested upon the shoulder of its neighbor, turning what should have been isolated, fragile timbers into an unbroken, buoyant structural continuum.

Traditional carpentry depended on complex mortise-and-tenon joints secured with dry wooden pegs, but sizing reciprocal frames purely by eye carries dangerous structural vulnerabilities. Because each beam rests on its neighbor with an eccentric vertical offset, the axial compression that naturally stabilizes a dome gets converted into intense localized bending and shear at every notched joint. If carpenters cut a notch too deep, they sever the long wood fibers that carry tensile bending stress, concentrating splitting forces perpendicular to the grain. Under the weight of heavy winter snow or shifting wind gusts, an unreinforced notch can shear cleanly along the grain line. Because the system is mutually dependent, the failure of a single lap joint triggers an immediate domino effect, destabilizing adjacent members and causing the entire reciprocal shell to snap downward in catastrophic elastic buckling. Modern engineers must therefore verify both local notch shear stresses and global lattice stability simultaneously.

Act II — How It Actually Works: Plain-Language Intuition to Governing Law (A.02)

Analogía Cotidiana y Guía de Controles

Everyday Analogy: Interlock four index fingers in a closed pinwheel loop, where each fingertip presses down on the knuckle of the next. Pressing downward in the center makes the circle tighten against your palms with surprising rigidity. However, if one knuckle slips sideways or if you press too hard near the delicate nail bed, the interlocking grip instantly unravels. Reciprocal timber frames balance this exact trade-off: mutual geometric support creates strength, but localized joint shearing must be rigorously reinforced.

How the Two Sliders Work in Plain Words: In the digital workshop below, you explore how two architectural sliders govern the stability of a reciprocal timber shell. The first slider adjusts the engagement ratio, which dictates how far along the length of each beam the neighboring timber rests. When you slide this value toward the outer tip, the central open eye of the pinwheel expands into a wide, airy skylight, but the bending leverage on each beam increases dramatically. Bringing the joint closer to the center stiffens the weave but crowds the wood members together. The second slider controls the downward shear force acting at the notched joint, representing the weight of snow, roofing tiles, and occupants pressing onto that connection. As this shear force climbs, it tests the shear strength of the wooden notch. Balancing these two settings keeps the timber safely within its natural structural capacity.

When gravity loads act on a curved reciprocal timber gridshell, the system transfers force through membrane compression along its diagonal lattice ribs. However, what threatens slender timber shells is not compressive crushing, but local joint splitting and global elastic buckling. The vertical offset e_z at each lap joint inherently produces an eccentric bending moment M_lap = V_lap * a * (1 - lambda_lap), as analyzed by Olga Popovic Larsen (DOI DOI 10.4324/9780080556000 [Scite ↗]) and Asefi and Bahremandi-Tolou (DOI DOI 10.1016/j.jobe.2019.100867 [Scite ↗]). The nonlinear coupling between engagement ratio lambda and lap shear force V_lap dictates the overall geometry and safety margin of the gridshell. Increasing the engagement ratio shifts the lap connection toward the mid-span of the receiving beam, which increases the vertical rise of the dome through cumulative eccentric camber. However, this same geometric expansion elongates the cantilever arm a, multiplying the eccentric bending moment that the wood fibers must resist in flexure. If the lap shear force surges under heavy snow accumulation, the shear stress at the notch root escalates nonlinearly. Without perpendicular-to-grain screw reinforcement, the timber splits longitudinally along the grain boundary. Furthermore, because timber is an orthotropic material with high longitudinal stiffness but low transverse shear resistance, slip within the semi-rigid joints softens the global shell lattice. As rotational joint stiffness degrades, the effective buckling length of each rib bay grows, lowering the global critical buckling threshold and precipitating snap-through instability.
