Act I — The Human Story & Historical Turning Point (M.01)

In the quiet winter of 1637, the French magistrate Pierre de Fermat sat beside a crackling hearth in Castres, studying the Greek geometry of Diophantus in Claude Bachet's Latin translation. Pausing over the eighth problem in the second book—which divided a given square into two smaller squares—Fermat reached for his quill and penned a provocative observation into the wide paper margin. He asserted that while squares combine with natural harmony, cubes can never dissolve into two cubes, nor fourth powers into fourth powers, nor any higher power indefinitely. He famously added that he had discovered a truly marvelous demonstration, yet the parchment border was too narrow to hold it. When Fermat passed away three decades later, leaving behind neither notes nor rough scratchwork, that tantalizing sentence became the most celebrated obsession in intellectual history, defying the greatest minds across Europe for more than three centuries.
