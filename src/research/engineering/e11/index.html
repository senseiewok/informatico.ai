Act I — Cool Fact & Human Story: Why a Rectangular Concrete Floor Slab Wastes 70% of Its Own Weight

Here is a staggering engineering fact: up to 70% of the concrete inside a conventional flat rectangular floor slab is not holding up the people, furniture, or lab equipment in the room—it is only there to hold up the dead weight of the concrete itself, because flat wooden formwork boxes are cheap to nail together. Cement manufacturing alone accounts for roughly 8% of global carbon dioxide emissions, a central World Economic Forum industrial decarbonization challenge. Yet 120 years ago, in 1904, Australian engineer Anthony G. M. Michell proved mathematically that minimum-weight structures never look like solid rectangular beams: they look like delicate, orthogonal spiderwebs of tension and compression curves (Hencky–Prandtl nets), identical to the spongy trabecular bone arches inside the human femur first described by Julius Wolff in 1892.

For a century, builders could not pour concrete into Michell's organic bone-like ribs because carpenter-built timber formwork would cost ten times more than the concrete saved.
