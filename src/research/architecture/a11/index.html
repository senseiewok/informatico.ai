Act I — Cool Fact & Human Story: How 50-Millimeter Stone Vaults Stand for Centuries Without a Single Gram of Steel

In the winter of 1864 in Glenlair, Scotland, the great polymath James Clerk Maxwell took a break from his groundbreaking equations on electromagnetism to gaze at a puzzle that had baffled stone masons for centuries. Standing inside ancient Gothic cathedrals like King's College Chapel in Cambridge, one looks up at soaring stone vaults that are proportionally thinner than a chicken eggshell, dry-stacked without tensile mortar or steel reinforcement, yet standing firm after half a millennium. Maxwell sketched a radical geometric insight on his notebook pages: the invisible flow of forces pushing through a stone structure can be drawn as a dual, mirror-image geometric polyhedron. Half a world away in 1913, Spanish master builder Rafael Guastavino used this compression principle to erect the famous Whispering Gallery and vaulted ceilings of Grand Central Terminal in New York City using lightweight terracotta tiles. These historical masterworks stood not through brute material strength, but through pure, mathematically perfected three-dimensional geometry.

In Plain Words · Extra-Easy Explanation

In plain words, think about how stone and concrete work. If you try to pull a stone apart like a piece of taffy, it cracks and fails almost immediately. But if you squeeze a stone tightly between your hands, it can support immense weight without breaking. Standard modern floors are built as flat concrete slabs. Because flat slabs bend and sag in the middle under gravity, the bottom half gets stretched, forcing builders to bury thousands of pounds of expensive, rust-prone steel rebar inside the concrete. Master vault builders avoided this problem completely by arching the floor upward into a shallow dome. When gravity presses down on an arch, the stone blocks wedge tightly against one another, funneling all the weight downward into pure compression. By using modern computers to shape these curved vaults in three dimensions, architects can build incredibly strong floors out of simple earth tiles or recycled brick, eliminating steel reinforcement entirely and slashing carbon emissions.

In Folio A.01 we traced Robert Hooke's 2D hanging chain and Jacques Heyman's plastic limit theorems (The Stone Skeleton, 1995, DOI DOI 10.1017/cbo9781107050310 [Scite ↗]). But real architectural spaces are three-dimensional, curved, and pierced by stairwells where simple two-dimensional slices fail. In 1864, James Clerk Maxwell discovered that a spatial structural framework is in exact static equilibrium if and only if it represents the projection of a polyhedral Airy stress surface, later generalized by C. R. Calladine (1978, DOI DOI 10.1016/0020-7683(78)90052-5 [Scite ↗]). In this three-dimensional polyhedral graphic statics, every joint in a stone vault corresponds to a closed cell in a reciprocal force polyhedron, where each strut's axial force equals the area of its dual polygonal face. At ETH Zurich, Philippe Block and the late Matthias Rippmann incorporated Maxwell's reciprocal geometry into open-source software (github.com/compas-dev/compas). This research enabled the Armadillo Vault—spanning sixteen meters from 399 dry-stacked limestone blocks with zero mortar—and funicular ribbed floor slabs that cut embodied concrete and carbon by over seventy percent without steel rebar.

For centuries, master masons relied on lodge rules of thumb and geometric templates to size stone arches and buttresses. While these empirical rules produced magnificent structures, they required immense intuition and could not safely predict how irregular, freeform, or asymmetric vaults would behave under complex live loads. When twentieth-century builders transitioned to flat reinforced-concrete slabs, they substituted computational certainty for material efficiency: pouring massive, heavy flat slabs packed with tensile steel was simpler to calculate than complex masonry curves. Today, building floor slabs consume over forty percent of the total concrete and embodied carbon in a typical high-rise building. Furthermore, embedded steel rebar inevitably oxidizes and corrodes when exposed to ambient moisture and carbonation over time, causing concrete spalling, cracking, and premature structural failure across modern infrastructure. Returning to unreinforced funicular compression vaults replaces this carbon-intensive, short-lived paradigm with ultra-durable geometries that endure for centuries while utilizing locally available, low-carbon masonry materials.

How the Two Sliders Work in Plain Words: To design a compression-only stone vault using this soap-bubble duality, you work with two primary structural knobs.
