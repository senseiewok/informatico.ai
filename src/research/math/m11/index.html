Act I — Cool Fact & Human Story: Parallel-Parking a Pencil in Zero Volume—and How It Unlocks Both Prime Numbers and Faster MRI Scans

In the turbulent winter of 1917 in Petrograd, Russian mathematician Abram Besicovitch pondered a geometric puzzle that defied ordinary physical intuition. If you place a one-inch sewing needle on a wooden table, how small can you make the region of wood the needle touches if you must rotate it completely around through three hundred sixty degrees? Everyone assumed that turning a needle required at least a modest circular clearing. Yet through an ingenious sequence of triangular splittings and overlapping shifts, Besicovitch proved that the total area touched can be made arbitrarily close to zero—smaller than a speck of dust! Pushing this paradox into three dimensions sparked the famous Kakeya Conjecture: even if a shape has zero volume while containing a needle pointing in every direction, can its intricate fractal dimension also shrink, or must it stubbornly fill all three dimensions? For over a century, that question defied the world's greatest harmonic analysts.
